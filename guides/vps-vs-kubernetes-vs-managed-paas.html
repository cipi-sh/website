Choosing Cipi

Cipi on a VPS, Kubernetes or a managed platform: how to choose

A fixed bill or a variable one, your server or someone else's. The honest way to choose is to put numbers on what the managed option buys you, then check whether you need it.

By Andrea PollastriUpdated November 24, 202612 min read

Share

There are four common ways to run a Laravel app in production. They differ less in what the app can do than in who runs the servers and how the bill is built. This guide compares them, then gives you three tests to decide, with your own numbers, whether a variable bill is worth its premium.

Many managed platforms are Kubernetes underneath (Ploi Cloud, for example, deploys to managed Kubernetes clusters): with a PaaS you pay someone to run that layer for you.

Self-hosted vs managed: who does what#

"Managed" doesn't mean "nothing to do", and "self-hosted" doesn't mean "do it all by hand". This is the honest split:

The rows that stay yours in every column (code, queries, deploys) are where most incidents come from. Managed platforms take away the infrastructure rows; Cipi automates most of the same rows, and leaves the decisions and the pager with you.

How the bills are built#

A fixed bill has one line: the server. Bigger traffic makes the server busier, not the invoice longer.

A variable bill multiplies prices by usage. A typical one has a base plan plus: compute per second per replica, database compute and storage, cache, bandwidth per GB, build minutes, log retention, sometimes per-seat fees. Published prices in November 2026, as examples (check the vendors' pages before deciding):

Fixed isn't frozen either: Hetzner raised its cloud prices twice in 2026. But a VPS price is one number, announced in advance, easy to compare across providers, and Cipi is the same on all of them.

Why a predictable bill is worth more than it looks#

You can quote it. An agency that bills clients a fixed hosting fee turns every variable line into margin risk. A company budget wants one number per year, not a range.

It grows with you, not with accidents. A variable bill grows with things you don't choose: an aggressive crawler, a bot pulling your images, a queue job stuck in a retry loop, an autoscaler that reaches its maximum during an attack. On a VPS these make the server slower and fire an alert; on usage billing they also make an invoice. Two terabytes of unexpected traffic at $0.09–0.10 per GB is about $180–200, in one month.

Spending limits cap the bill, not the cause. When the cap is reached, the app degrades or stops: the same thing a VPS does when it's full. The difference is that, up to the cap, you paid more per unit of capacity.

Every decision stops being a pricing decision. Add a queue worker, keep logs longer, cache more, run a nightly report: on a fixed bill you decide on technical grounds. On a variable bill each choice is also a cost forecast.

Leaving stays cheap. A Cipi server is standard Ubuntu with standard packages; another provider is one cipi sync away. Once an app depends on a platform's serverless database, managed queues and storage, leaving costs engineering time, and that cost belongs in the comparison.

Three tests: what must a variable bill buy you?#

Call F the fixed monthly cost of the Cipi setup (the VPS sized for your peak, backup storage, maybe a standby server) and V your realistic monthly bill on the managed platform, from its own calculator. The premium is P = V − F. Here's what P has to pay for.

Test 1 — Time: is it buying hours?#

text
break-even hours per month = P ÷ your hourly cost
Premium PHourly cost €60Hourly cost €90
€50 / month0.8 h0.6 h
€200 / month3.3 h2.2 h
€500 / month8.3 h5.6 h

The managed platform must save you at least that many hours every month. Count honestly: on a Cipi server patching, certificate renewals, backups, verification and alerting run by themselves. What's left is reading alerts, the upgrades you decide to do, and incidents. If that's below the break-even, the premium isn't buying time; it's buying comfort, which is fine, as long as you know it.

Test 2 — Elasticity: do your peaks pay for it?#

You size a VPS for your peak; a usage bill charges your average. Call m the platform's markup: the monthly price of an always-on instance on the platform divided by the price of a VPS with the same vCPU and RAM.

text
usage billing is cheaper only when  peak ÷ average  >  m
Your trafficPeak ÷ averageUsually cheaper
Business app, office hours2–3×VPS
E-commerce, normal weeks2–4×VPS
Ticket sales, launches, TV spots10–50× during the eventUsage billing, or a VPS resized for the event
Side project, idle most of the dayvery high (average near zero)Scale-to-zero, unless it shares a VPS with other apps
Staging and previewsvery highOn Cipi they share an existing server at no extra cost

These are typical shapes; measure yours. sudo cipi metrics show --app=shop --range=30d gives the minimum, average and peak of an app's CPU and requests per minute over the last month.

Two corrections make the test fairer. A Cipi server hosts many apps for one price, so compare per server, not per app: ten small sites on one VPS have a marginal cost close to zero. And a VPS can be resized for a known event and back, which turns a predictable peak into a predictable one-off cost.

Test 3 — Risk: is it buying availability?#

text
justified if  12 × P  ≤  (cost of one hour down) × (hours of downtime it avoids per year)

With P = €200 (€2,400 a year) and an hour of downtime costing you €300, the platform must avoid 8 hours of downtime a year that you would otherwise have. Compare like with like: single-server commitments from VPS providers commonly sit between 99.5% and 99.99% (Hetzner's is 99.9%, about 8.8 hours a year), so check what the managed platform commits to on the plan you'd actually buy. What changes the number more is your recovery time: on Cipi, health checks with automatic rollback undo a bad release in seconds, the monitor and the heartbeat tell you the server is in trouble, and a warm standby fed by cipi sync every few hours is a DNS change away.

If your downtime costs thousands per hour and you need multi-zone failover without designing it yourself, test 3 says "managed", and you should listen.

The verdict#

A variable bill earns its premium whenMeasured as
It saves more hours than it costsTest 1: hours saved × hourly cost > P
Your peaks are much higher than your averageTest 2: peak ÷ average > m
It avoids downtime you can't avoid otherwiseTest 3: downtime avoided × cost per hour > 12 × P
Nobody can own a server, and you won't hire or contract someone who canA team decision, not a number

If none of these holds, the fixed bill wins, usually by a wide margin, and keeps winning as you grow, because a busier server costs the same.

Kubernetes: when it pays off#

Kubernetes is an excellent platform for running many services, owned by many teams, on a shared pool of machines. For a Laravel monolith with queues and a scheduler, most of its value goes unused and most of its cost remains.

Kubernetes makes sense whenIt's overhead when
Dozens of services in several languagesOne or a few Laravel or PHP apps
A platform team already runs clusters for the companyThe developers would also become the platform team
You need bin-packing and self-healing across many nodesOne or two servers carry the load
Images are built once in CI and promoted unchanged through environmentsgit push and a release folder are enough
A company standard requires itNobody asked for it

What you build yourself for Laravel on Kubernetes: images per release, web and worker Deployments, the scheduler (a CronJob or one replica of schedule:work), migrations as a Job before the rollout, sessions and cache in Valkey or Redis, uploads on object storage, Horizon and Reverb processes, ingress and certificates. And what you pay: the control plane where it's billed, at least two or three nodes for availability, load balancers, NAT gateways and traffic on the big clouds, plus regular cluster upgrades to stay on supported versions (Kubernetes ships about three minor versions a year; on EKS a version leaves standard support after 14 months and the cluster fee rises sixfold).

Cipi deliberately doesn't use containers on the server (why). If containers are your standard, a self-hosted Docker platform such as Coolify or Dokku is a lighter step than Kubernetes.

Questions

Is a VPS always cheaper?

For steady traffic, almost always: one price covers app, database, cache and bandwidth. For apps that are idle most of the time, or whose peaks are many times their average, usage billing with scale-to-zero can win. The elasticity test tells you which case you're in.

Isn't my time more expensive than the price difference?

Sometimes. That's why the first test in this guide converts the premium into hours. If the managed platform doesn't save you that many hours a month, the premium isn't buying time.

What if the VPS provider raises its prices?

It happens: Hetzner raised cloud prices twice in 2026. The difference is that a VPS price is one number, announced in advance, and Cipi is the same on every provider, so moving is a cipi sync away.

Do spending limits fix unpredictable bills?

They cap the bill, not the cause. When the cap is hit, the app degrades or stops, which is what a VPS does at full capacity, but you've paid more per unit of capacity up to that point.

Should a Laravel monolith run on Kubernetes?

Rarely on its own. Kubernetes pays off when many services and teams share a platform that someone already operates. For one app with queues and a scheduler, it's mostly overhead.

Can I mix them?

Yes. Common mixes: Cipi for production with a managed database for the data, Cipi for Laravel apps next to a Kubernetes platform for other services, or a managed platform for previews and Cipi for production.

Does Cipi do autoscaling or high availability?

No autoscaling: you size the server. For availability: monitoring, health checks with auto-rollback, verified backups and a warm standby with cipi sync. If you need multi-zone failover without thinking about it, a managed platform is the better fit.
